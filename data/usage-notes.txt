# Usage notes written for this dictionary: when to use a word, when not to, mix-ups, and regional differences.
# headword | note
a'ready | Everyday all over Scotland; say it where English says "already". Often shortened to "aready" in fast Glasgow speech.
a'richt | The everyday Scots "all right" – as a greeting (A'richt?) or an answer. Glasgow often says "awright"; both are fine.
aboot | Use it anywhere English has "about" or "around". Pairs naturally with "whit's it aw aboot?"; don't write "aboot" for the number sense "approximately" in formal Scots – "roond aboot" is more natural.
abraid | A strong word for a telling-off; use it of someone giving another a row, not for mild criticism.
ae | Means "one" only before a noun (ae day, ae thing). On its own as "the one", say "yin" or "ane" instead – "ae" can't stand alone.
aff | The normal Scots "off": "get aff the bus", "he's aff his heid". Don't confuse with "aft" (often).
affrontit | Use it when someone has shown you up or you are mortified. For extra force say "black affrontit". It isn't "affronted" in the English sense of offended.
afore | Everyday for "before" in time or place. "Afore lang" = before long. Don't mix it up with "aforehaun" (beforehand).
again | In Scots it can mean "against" (lean it again the wa) or "by the time" (again he comes). The plain "again" (once more) is said "agane/agen".
agent | In Scotland a "law agent" or just "agent" is your solicitor. Not used for a sales agent in this sense.
ain | Always "yer ain", "ma ain", "oor ain" – own. Don't use it without a possessive in front.
airm | The Scots form of "arm" (body part). For the weapon sense English "arms" is usual.
airn | Iron, both the metal and the thing for pressing clothes. "Airn the shirt" is everyday.
aither | Either. Glasgow speech often says "either" or "eether"; "aither" is more general Scots.
alangside | Beside, next to. Natural in speech; "alang wi" is the usual way to say "along with".
alloo | Allow. Common in older speech – "ye're no alloo'd" sounds very Scots.
amang | Among. Use it in all registers; "amang the hills" sounds natural and not old-fashioned.
an | The everyday "and". In writing Scots, drop the d: "fish an chips".
aneath | Beneath, underneath. Glasgow tends to say "unner"; "aneath" is a bit more traditional.
anither | Another. Everyday everywhere. Glasgow sometimes says "anurra".
apen | Open. Heard in the East and North; Glasgow usually just says "open".
auld | Old. One of the best-known Scots words; use it for people and things. "Ma auld man" = my dad (or husband).
ava | At all, used after a negative: "nae bother ava", "no ava". Don't use it in a positive sentence.
awa | Away. Also as a dismissive "awa ye go!" or "awa an bile yer heid". Don't confuse with "awa" meaning dead (he's awa) – context tells.
aye | Yes – the most common Scots word of all. Also "always" in older or written Scots (aye the same). "Aye right" is sarcastic: it means no.
bairn | Child, used all over Scotland. In Glasgow "wean" is more common; "bairn" is the East and North word.
bairnheid | Childhood. A bit literary – in speech folk more often say "when Ah wis wee".
baith | Both. Use it just like English "both": "baith o ye".
baloo | A lullaby or lulling word; mostly in old songs and when soothing a baby.
bane | Bone. Shows up in sayings: "rickle o banes", "near the bane" (stingy).
baw | A ball. Careful: in Glasgow slang it also means testicle, so "bawheid" and "bawbag" are insults.
bealin | Raging, furious. Very Glasgow/West. Stronger than "angry" – you're bealin when you're boiling with temper.
begrutten | Tear-stained from crying. A softer, older word – nice in writing.
behoochie | A joking word for the backside; fine with children, not rude.
ben | The inner room ("come ben the hoose") or a mountain (Ben Nevis). "But an ben" is the classic two-roomed cottage.
bere | A hardy old barley still grown in Orkney for bere bannocks. Not the animal (that's "bear").
bide | To live or stay: "whaur dae ye bide?" Also "bide a wee" = wait a moment. Don't use for "abide" (tolerate) – that's "thole".
big | As a verb it means "to build" (he biggit a dyke). As an adjective it is the same as English big.
biggin | A building, especially a country one. More rural and traditional than city speech.
birk | Birch tree. Common in place names (Birkhill) and songs.
bit | Before a noun it means little: "a bit lassie". Don't confuse with "a bit" (a piece).
black | In Scots "black" also means filthy (yer hauns are black) or acts as "utterly" in "black affrontit". Plain colour sense is the same as English.
black affrontit | A set phrase for utterly mortified – use it when you've been shown up badly. Don't swap "black" for another word.
blae | Blue-grey, the colour of cold skin or slate. "Blaeberry" is the bilberry.
blate | Shy or bashful – used of children and of anyone slow to come forward. "Dinnae be blate" = help yourself.
blaw | To blow, or to boast ("he's aye blawin"). "Blaw yer nose" is everyday.
blether | To chat or talk rubbish; also a chatterbox ("she's a richt blether"). A friendly word, not an insult unless said sharply.
bluid | Blood. Used in all registers; "bluidy" as a swear is the same as English.
boak | To retch or the urge to be sick: "it gies me the boak" = it makes me feel sick. Very common in Glasgow.
body | A person: "a body cannae get peace". "Naebody" and "somebody" come from it.
bonnie | Pretty, fine, lovely – of people, places and days. Known everywhere; not used ironically as much as "braw".
boo | A bow (bending). Rhymes with "moo"; don't confuse with the ghost "boo!".
bourtree | Elder tree. Rural and traditional; city folk rarely use it now.
brae | A slope or hillside; also a street that runs up a hill. Common in place names (Braehead).
braw | Fine, great, lovely – of weather, people, clothes or news. Strongest in the East and North; Glasgow speakers understand it but use it less in everyday talk.
breenge | To charge in headlong without thinking. Common in the West: "he jist breenged in".
breid | Bread. Everyday; "breid an butter".
bricht | Bright. The "ch" is the throat sound as in loch.
brig | Bridge. In place names (Brig o' Doon) and older speech; city Scots often say "bridge".
brither | Brother. Everyday in most areas; Glasgow sometimes says "brurra".
broo | The dole (unemployment office) in modern slang ("on the broo"), or the brow, or soup. Context decides.
broon | Brown: "broon sauce", "broon breid". Everyday everywhere.
bucket | In Glasgow the "bucket" is the rubbish bin (and the bin lorry men are the "scaffies"). Not a pail.
bud | North East: "had to" or "must" – "he bud gang". Not used in Glasgow.
buik | Book. Pronounced with a short "i" sound in many areas.
burn | A stream. Very common everywhere in Scotland; not a burn from fire in this sense.
buss | A bush. Traditional; city speech often says "bush".
but | As a noun it is the outer room of a cottage (but an ben). Glasgow speech also puts "but" at the end of a sentence to mean "though".
byordinar | Extraordinary, unusual. A bit literary but still heard in the North East.
byre | A cowshed. Farming word, still used in the country.
ca | To call, to drive or push, or to knock: "ca canny" (go carefully), "ca the door". Watch the many meanings.
ca' canny | Go carefully or go easy. Used as a warning or a goodbye.
cairry | Carry. "Cairry-oot" is takeaway (especially drink).
caller | Fresh (of fish or air). "Caller herrin" is the old street cry. Cool rather than cold.
carnaptious | Crabbit and quarrelsome. A fun word; use it of someone who argues about everything.
cauf | A calf (animal or back of the leg).
cauld | Cold. One of the most common Scots words. In some rivers "cauld" is also a weir.
cauldrife | Feeling the cold easily; chilly. Use it of people who always need a jumper.
chap | To knock (at a door): "chap the door". Not a man (that's the English sense).
chaumer | A room, especially a farm worker's bothy room. Rural and traditional.
chowk | To choke. Borders form; elsewhere "choke" or "worry" is used.
claik | Gossip, or the honk of geese. "A claik" can be a gossiping person.
clarty | Dirty, sticky, muddy – for messy hands, clothes or houses. Everyday.
clean | As an adverb it means completely: "clean daft". The plain adjective is the same as English.
cleek | A hook, or to link arms or hook something. "Cleek yer airm" = link your arm.
clock | Of a hen, to brood or sit on eggs. Not a timepiece in this sense.
close | The entry and stair of a tenement; in Edinburgh also a narrow lane. "Up the close" is everyday Glasgow.
cludgie | A toilet – informal and a bit jokey. Fine among friends, not for formal use.
cod | A pillow. Old-fashioned; mostly heard in the North and rural areas.
coff | To buy. Old word; today folk say "buy".
coo | Cow. Everyday; "Hielan coo" is the Highland cow.
coup | To tip over or spill ("ye'll coup it"), or a rubbish tip/messy place ("this room's a coup"). Everyday.
crabbit | Bad-tempered, grumpy. One of the most used Scots words for mood.
craitur | A creature; said with pity of a person ("the puir craitur").
crine | To shrivel or shrink, of food or old folk. Traditional.
crivvens | A mild "oh my!" – familiar from comic strips. Light and old-fashioned.
dafty | A silly person. Mild and often affectionate; harsher words are "eejit" or "numpty".
daimen | Now and then, occasional. Mostly literary (Burns).
dander | A slow stroll: "gaun for a dander". Everyday, especially in the West and Ulster.
daud | A big lump ("a daud o breid") or a thump. Everyday in the North East.
daur | Dare: "Ah daurna" = I daren't, "daur ye!" = just you dare! Traditional but still heard.
dautie | A darling or pet. Affectionate; traditional.
dee | To die. Everyday in speech; "deid" is the adjective.
deid | Dead. Also intensifier "deid easy" (very easy) in Glasgow.
deif | Deaf. "Are ye deif?" is a common rebuke.
deil | The devil. In sayings: "the deil tak ye", "deil a bit" (not at all).
deuk | Duck. Pronounced like "dyook".
dicht | To wipe: "dicht yer mooth". Everyday in the North and East.
ding | To knock or beat; "dingin doon" = pouring with rain.
dominie | A schoolmaster. Old-fashioned; used humorously now.
donner | To daze. More common as "donnert" (dazed, stupid).
doo | A dove or pigeon. "Doocot" is the dovecote. Also a term of endearment.
dook | To dip or swim; "dookin for aipples" at Halloween. Everyday.
doon | Down. Everywhere. "Doon the watter" = a trip down the Clyde.
dover | To doze off. Gentle word: "Ah jist dovered aff".
dowie | Sad, dreary. Mostly literary or rural.
dozen | To daze. More common as "dozent" (dopey, half-asleep). Not the number twelve here.
draucht | A draught (of air or drink).
dree | To endure. Nearly always in "dree yer weird" (put up with your lot).
dreich | Dull, grey and wet – the classic word for Scottish weather. Also means tedious.
droon | Drown: "Ah near drooned". Everyday everywhere; also used for over-watering ("ye've drooned the plant") or too much sauce.
dug | Dog. Everyday in the West; the North East says "dog" or "doug". As a verb (Glasgow) "dug it" means skipped it.
dunch | To nudge or shove; also a punch. More East and North.
dwam | A daze or daydream: "in a dwam". Everyday and well-loved.
dwine | To fade or waste away, of people or plants.
dyke | A stone wall (dry-stane dyke). Not a sea bank as in Dutch – in Scotland it's mostly a wall.
efter | After. Everyday. "Efter the day" = after today.
eneuch | Enough. Glasgow often just says "enough" or "enuff"; "eneuch" is more traditional.
erse | Backside (rude). Use with care.
ettle | To intend or try. Traditional; heard in the North and in writing.
fa | To fall; past "fell" or "fa'n". "Dinnae fa!" is everyday.
fair | Before a word it means "really, completely": "fair puggled", "fair scunnert". Not the English "just".
faither | Father. Glasgow everyday speech often uses "da" or "faither" jokingly.
fash | To bother or worry: "dinnae fash yersel" = don't worry. Very common and friendly.
fashious | Troublesome, fussy, hard to please.
fause | False. Also "fause teeth" for dentures.
faut | Fault: "it's no ma faut". Everyday; "fautless" = faultless.
feart | Afraid. Everyday everywhere: "Ah'm no feart".
feartie | A scaredy-cat. Children's word; playful.
fecht | A fight or a struggle; "it's a fecht" = it's hard going.
ferm | Farm. "Fermtoun" is the farm settlement.
fermer | Farmer. Everyday in the country. Don't confuse with "ferm" (the farm itself).
ferntickle | A freckle. Traditional; nice in writing.
fernyear | Last year. Old but still heard in the North.
fire-flaucht | Lightning flash. Literary.
first-fit | To be the first visitor after the bells at New Year, bringing coal, shortbread or whisky.
fit | Foot; also "kick". In the North East "fit" means "what" (fit like?). Never means "able".
flair | Floor: "oan the flair". Everyday everywhere; not "flair" (talent) as in English.
flane | An arrow. Old; rare in speech.
flee | A fly (insect) or to fly. Context tells which.
fleein Nansie | A humorous name for flu. Old-fashioned fun.
fleg | A fright: "ye gied me a fleg". Everyday.
flichter | To flutter or flicker. Lovely in writing.
flit | To move house. "A munelicht flit" is leaving without paying the rent.
forjeskit | Worn out with work. Traditional; "wabbit" or "puggled" are more common now.
fortnicht | Fortnight – two weeks. Everyday; the "ch" is the throat sound as in loch.
fou | Full, or drunk ("fou as a puggie"). Also a bushel. Context tells.
fower | Four. Everyday everywhere; say it to rhyme with "power".
fremmit | Strange, foreign, unfriendly. Traditional.
fummle | To fumble or handle clumsily. Everyday; "fummlin aboot" = messing about with your hands.
gate | A way or road ("gang yer ain gate"). In street names (Gallowgate) it means street, not a gate.
gawsie | Plump and cheerful-looking. Used kindly, especially of babies (Burns uses it of the haggis).
gie | Give. Everyday: "gie's it", "gie it laldy".
gie it laldy | Go at something with all your energy – singing, dancing, working. Glasgow favourite.
gimmer | A young ewe; also used as an insult for an old woman. Careful.
gin | "If" in older Scots (gin ye gang = if you go). Today folk say "if". Don't confuse with the drink.
girr | A hoop (child's toy). Old.
glaikit | Gormless, vacant-looking. Very common insult in Glasgow; mild-ish.
gled | A kite (bird) or a greedy person.
gleg | Quick, sharp-witted. "Gleg in the uptak" = quick to understand.
glisk | A glimpse. Traditional and North East.
gloamin | Twilight. Poetic but still used; "roamin in the gloamin" from the old song.
gonnae no | Glasgow for "please don't" – "Gonnae no dae that." The answer is often "How no?"
gowan | Daisy. Traditional and poetic.
gowd | Gold. Traditional.
gowden | Golden. Literary.
gowf | Golf; also a cuff on the ear.
gravat | A scarf. Old-fashioned; some older folk still say it.
greet | To cry. Everyday everywhere: "stop yer greetin". A "greetin face" is a moaner. Don't confuse with English "greet" (welcome).
guddle | A mess (what a guddle!) or to catch fish by hand. Everyday.
guid | Good. Pronounced "gid" in Glasgow, "gweed" in the North East.
guid-brither | Brother-in-law. "Guid" here means "in-law", not good.
guid-dochter | Daughter-in-law. In these family words "guid" means "in-law", not "good" – a common learner mix-up. (Some older speakers also use it for a stepdaughter.)
guid-faither | Father-in-law. "Guid" here means "in-law". Some older speakers also use it for a stepfather, so check which is meant.
guid-mither | Mother-in-law. "Guid" here means "in-law"; in older speech it can also mean stepmother.
guid-sister | Sister-in-law. Traditional and still heard among older speakers; younger folk usually say "sister-in-law".
guid-son | Son-in-law. Traditional; "guid" means "in-law", not "good".
guidman | A husband, or the man of the house. Traditional.
gully | A big knife. Traditional and North East.
gyte | Mad, out of your mind: "gaun gyte". Everyday in the North East.
haar | A cold sea fog on the east coast. The West rarely uses it.
hairst | Harvest; also autumn in older speech.
haiverel | A babbler or halfwit. Traditional.
hame | Home. One of the most common words: "gaun hame".
haud | Hold: "haud on", "haud yer wheesht". Everyday everywhere.
hauf | Half. In a pub "a hauf" is a measure of whisky; "a hauf an a hauf" is a whisky and a half pint.
haver | Oats. Not the same as "haiver" (to talk nonsense).
heich | High. The "ch" is the throat sound.
heid | Head. Everyday: "use yer heid", "lost the heid".
help ma boab | A mild exclamation of surprise or exasperation, made famous by Oor Wullie. Light and humorous.
hert | Heart. Everyday in speech and song: "bless yer hert", "ma hert's broke". Glasgow often keeps it as "hert" too.
heuch | A cliff or steep bank. Traditional, Borders.
hing | To hang. In Glasgow "hing" also means "thing". Context tells.
hirple | To limp or hobble. Everyday and expressive.
hoo | How. In Glasgow "how" is often used for "why": "how no?" = why not?
hoose | House. Everyday everywhere: "come roond tae the hoose". The plural is "hooses"; "hoosie" is a playhouse or a little house.
howff | A favourite haunt, especially a pub. East coast favourite.
howk | To dig (tatties, holes). Everyday.
hunner | Hundred. "Hunners" means lots: "there wis hunners o folk".
hurcheon | Hedgehog. Old and rural.
ilka | Every, each. Traditional; "ilka day" = every day.
ilkaday | Everyday, ordinary: "ilkaday claes".
ill | Bad or harmful in Scots, not just sick: "ill-trickit" (mischievous). "Ill" for sick is also used.
ingleneuk | The fireside corner. Cosy, traditional word.
ither | Other. Everyday; "each ither" = each other.
jalouse | To suspect or guess: "Ah jalouse he's lyin". Nothing to do with jealousy.
jeely | Jam (or jelly). "Jeely piece" = jam sandwich.
jobbie | A bit of poo – childish rather than rude. Glasgow uses "jobbie" for anything worthless.
jotter | A school exercise book. "Get yer jotters" = you're sacked.
jow | To toll a bell. Traditional.
keek | A peek or to peek: "gie's a keek". Everyday.
ken | To know: "dae ye ken?" East and North everyday word. In Glasgow "know" is more common, but "ken" is understood everywhere.
kenspeckle | Well known, easily recognised: "a kenspeckle figure".
kent | Known, as in "weel kent". Also a shepherd's staff.
kich | Filth or rubbish (rude). Not for polite company.
kichie | Dirty (rude). Childish word.
kinkhoast | Whooping cough. Old.
kintra | Country. Literary or old speech.
kirk | Church. Everyday: "the Kirk" is the Church of Scotland.
kirkyaird | Churchyard, the graveyard round a kirk. Everyday in the country; city folk may say "cemetery" for the bigger ones.
kittlin | Kitten. Traditional.
knicht | Knight. Mostly literary.
lair | Learning. Also a burial plot ("a lair in the cemetery") – watch the two meanings.
lammer | Amber. Traditional.
lang | Long. Everyday everywhere: "a lang day", "no lang noo". "Lang may yer lum reek" is a good-luck wish.
lang syne | Long ago, old times – best known from "Auld Lang Syne". Mostly in songs and writing.
lassie | A girl. Everyday everywhere; "lass" is the plain form.
lauch | Laugh. The "ch" is the throat sound.
laverock | Skylark. Poetic.
leal | Loyal. Literary ("the land o the leal" = heaven).
leid | A language: "the Scots leid". Also lead (metal). Becoming more common again in writing about Scots.
licht | Light. Everyday; "lichts" also means lungs.
links | Sandy ground by the sea, now mostly golf links.
littlin | A little child. Affectionate.
loch | A lake or sea inlet. Say the "ch" as a throat sound – never "lock".
loon | A boy in the North East (Doric). Not "loony" – it's a normal word there.
losh | A mild exclamation (from "Lord"). Old-fashioned.
luik | Look. Pronounced "luke" or "lyuk".
lum | Chimney: "lang may yer lum reek" = may you live long and well.
ma | My. Glasgow everyday; also "mum" ("ma maw").
mairch | A boundary; to share a boundary. Also the month (spelt March).
mairry | Marry: "they're gettin mairrit". Everyday; the past form is "mairrit", not "marriet".
maist | Most: "maist folk". Everyday everywhere. "Maistly" = mostly.
maistly | Mostly, nearly: "it's maistly done". Everyday; "maist" on its own means most.
mak | Make: "mak a cup o tea". Everyday everywhere; past forms "made" or "makkit".
maukin | A hare. Rural.
maun | Must. Traditional; Glasgow speech says "huv tae".
maunna | Mustn't. Traditional.
meenit | Minute (of time): "gie's a meenit". Everyday everywhere. Not the adjective "minute" (tiny) – that's "wee".
mercat | Market. Mostly in "mercat cross" and town names.
messages | The shopping. "Gaun for the messages" is everyday; in English "messages" means texts or notes.
micht | Might. Say the "ch" as in loch. Glasgow speech often says "might" or "mibbe" instead.
midnicht | Midnight. The "ch" is the throat sound; Glasgow often says "midnight".
mim | Prim and proper. "Mim-moued" = prim-mouthed.
mind | To remember: "Ah mind it fine". Also "to look after" (mind the wean) and "be careful" (mind yer heid) – context tells which.
mingin | Disgusting, stinking, or ugly. Strong and very common Glasgow word.
minnie | Mum (child's word). North and East.
mirk | Darkness. Literary or rural.
mischanter | A mishap. Traditional.
misleared | Badly brought up, ill-mannered. Traditional.
mony | Many: "mony a mickle maks a muckle".
moose | Mouse. Everyday; the plural is "mice" or "meece". Don't mix up with "moose" the big deer – in Scots that's just the mouse.
morn | Morning. "The morn" means tomorrow – a classic mix-up for learners.
muir | Moor, open heather land. Everyday in the country and in place names (Muirhead). Pronounced "mure" or "meer".
munelicht | Moonlight. Lovely in speech and song; "munelicht flittin" = sneaking off at night without paying rent.
naebody | Nobody: "naebody's in". Everyday everywhere; Glasgow also says "naebdy".
naither | Neither: "me naither". Everyday; Glasgow often says "neether" or "nayther".
nane | None: "there's nane left". Everyday; "nane the waur" = none the worse.
ned | A young thug. Glasgow and Edinburgh; disapproving.
nicht | Night or evening. "The nicht" means tonight – a common mix-up for learners. The "ch" is the throat sound as in loch.
nieve | Fist. Traditional; Glasgow says "fist".
no ava | Not at all: "it's no ava bad". Traditional and North East; Glasgow more often says "no at aw".
noo | Now. "The noo" = just now (or later). "Cheerio the noo" = bye for now.
nyaff | A small, irritating person. Glasgow insult.
onybody | Anybody: "is onybody in?". Everyday; Glasgow often "anybody" or "emdy".
onywhere | Anywhere. Everyday; "onywhere ye like".
oor | Our ("oor hoose") or hour ("an oor late"). Same spelling, so let context decide.
oot | Out: "get oot!", "oot the windae". Everyday everywhere; "ootside" = outside.
ootcome | Outcome, result. Used in more formal Scots writing; in speech folk often just say "how it turned oot".
ower | Over; also "too" (ower big). Learners often miss the "too" meaning.
oxter | Armpit; or to give someone your arm. Everyday.
oy | Grandchild. North East and Highland.
paidle | To paddle in water, or a hoe for weeding. "Gaun a paidle" at the beach is everyday.
paitrick | Partridge. Country word; city speakers seldom need it.
parritch | Porridge: "a bowl o parritch". Everyday and traditional; often treated as plural ("they're guid parritch").
partan | Crab (East coast). "Partan bree" is crab soup.
peerie | Small (Shetland and Orkney). Elsewhere "wee" is used. A "peerie" in Glasgow is a spinning top.
peety | Pity: "it's a peety". Everyday; "whit a peety" = what a shame.
pend | An arched passageway through a building into a back court. Everyday in Glasgow and Edinburgh tenement areas.
pey | Pay: "pey the rent". Everyday in the Borders and East; Glasgow says "pay".
piece | A sandwich or packed lunch: "a jeely piece". Not just "a piece of" something.
pish | Rude: urine; rubbish ("that's pish"). Avoid in polite company.
pit | Put. Everyday: "pit the kettle on".
plook | A spot or pimple. "Plook on a plinth" is a joke for a pretentious person.
ploom | Plum. Everyday in the North and East; Glasgow usually says "plum".
polis | Police. Stress the first syllable: PO-lis.
pouther | Powder: "washin pouther". Everyday among older speakers.
powny | Pony. Everyday; "Shetland powny".
preen | A pin. "Preen yersel" = dress up.
prent | Print. Used in Scots writing; in speech folk usually say "print".
press | A cupboard. Not a printing press in this sense.
puddock | A frog or toad.
puir | Poor (to be pitied, or without money).
pushion | Poison: "rat pushion". Everyday among older speakers; also of a horrible person.
quean | A young woman. In the North East "quine" is the everyday form.
ramstam | Reckless, rushing in headlong: "a ramstam lad". Use it for someone who acts without thinking.
ream | Cream (on milk). Rural and traditional; city speakers say "cream".
reek | Smoke, or to stink: "it's reekin". "Auld Reekie" is Edinburgh.
reekie | Smoky. Best known in "Auld Reekie" (Edinburgh); otherwise rare in speech.
reid | Red: "a reid face". Everyday everywhere; "reid-heidit" = red-haired.
richt | Right, correct; also "really" before a word ("richt guid"). "No richt in the heid" = not all there.
rickle o banes | A very thin person – used with some sympathy.
rift | To burp: "rift efter yer denner". Everyday among older speakers; also a burp itself.
rin | Run: "rin fur it!". Everyday; past is "ran" or "rin".
roond | Round: "come roond". Everyday everywhere; also "roond aboot" = around.
rump | To clean someone out (of money). Slang.
sab | Sob: "sabbin her hert oot". Everyday; stronger than "greet" (cry).
sae | So: "sae cauld", "sae ah went hame". Everyday everywhere; Glasgow also says "that" for so ("Ah wis that tired").
saft | Soft; also daft ("dinnae be saft") or wet weather ("a saft day").
saikless | Innocent. Traditional.
saip | Soap. Everyday among older speakers; "saip an watter".
sair | Sore. "Sair heid" = headache; "sair fecht" = hard struggle.
sark | Shirt (or a slip). Traditional; you'll hear it in songs, and "cutty sark" is a short shift.
saunt | Saint. Used in Scots writing and in place names; folk usually say "saint".
saut | Salt: "saut an vinegar". Everyday; also "sharp words" or punishment.
sax | Six. Everyday in the North and East; Glasgow usually says "six".
scrieve | To write. Old word now revived in writing about Scots.
scunner | To sicken; "a scunner" is something annoying. "Scunnert" = fed up. Very common.
sel | Self: masel, yersel.
shae | Shoe. Plural "shuin" or "shoon" in older speech; Glasgow says "shoe".
shaw | Show: "shaw us". Everyday among older speakers; also a small wood in place names.
sheuch | A ditch, drain or gutter. Everyday in the country; "in the sheuch" = in trouble.
shilpit | Puny, thin and pale-looking. Use it of someone who looks underfed or ill.
siller | Money, or silver. Traditional; Glasgow slang for money is "dosh" or "poppy".
simmer | Summer. Everyday in speech and song: "simmer holidays".
skaith | Harm or damage: "nae skaith done". Traditional, but still heard in the North.
skelf | A splinter, especially of wood in your finger. Everyday; also a very thin person.
skelp | A smack, especially on the bottom: "ye'll get a skelp". Everyday; also a big piece ("a skelp o land").
sklent | To slant, or to look sideways. Traditional; "sklent-eed" = squinting.
sleekit | Sly, cunning. Famous from Burns's "wee sleekit cowrin tim'rous beastie". Everyday insult.
slidder | To slide or slither. Everyday on icy days.
smirr | Light drizzle. A great weather word.
snaw | Snow. Everyday everywhere: "snaw on the hills".
sneck | A latch, or to latch a door: "sneck the door". Everyday in the country.
snod | Neat and tidy. Traditional; nice for a well-kept house.
sodger | Soldier. Everyday among older speakers; "play at sodgers".
soop | Sweep: "soop the flair". Everyday; in curling, the sweepers "soop" the ice.
sooth | South: "the sooth side". Everyday; "Sooth o the Border" = England.
spaewife | A fortune-teller. Traditional; you'll meet it in stories rather than everyday talk.
speerit | Spirit. Used in Scots writing; in speech folk often say "spirit".
speir | To ask: "speir at him". Traditional; Glasgow says "ask".
stane | Stone; also a weight of 14 pounds. Everyday everywhere.
starn | Star. Mostly in poems and songs; in speech folk say "star".
steer | Stir, fuss: "whit a steer".
steid | A place or site, especially of an old building. Traditional.
stert | Start: "stert noo". Everyday everywhere; "stertit" = started.
sterve | Starve; also to freeze ("Ah'm stervin").
stot | Bounce: "stot the baw". "Stotious" is drunk.
strae | Straw. Everyday in the country; "strae hat".
straik | To stroke (a cat), or a stroke/blow. Everyday among older speakers.
stramash | An uproar or rammy. Everyday.
stravaig | To wander about with no real aim: "stravaigin the toon". Lovely, still-used word.
sundoun | Sunset. Traditional and rural; city speakers say "sunset".
sunsheen | Sunshine. Everyday in speech; "a bit o sunsheen".
swick | To cheat or swindle: "he swickit me". Traditional; Glasgow says "rip aff".
swither | To be in two minds: "Ah'm in a swither". Very common.
syne | Then, afterwards; "lang syne" = long ago. Traditional.
tae | To; too; toe. Very common – context tells.
taid | Toad. Everyday in the country; "puddock" is used for frogs and toads too.
tait | A small tuft or bit: "a tait o oo". Traditional.
tak | Take: "tak it". Everyday everywhere; past "took" or "taen".
tapsalteerie | Upside down, in a muddle: "aw tapsalteerie". Fun, well-known word.
thegither | Together: "aw thegither". Everyday everywhere.
thocht | Thought; also worry. Everyday; "a wee thocht" = a small amount.
thrang | Busy, crowded: "thrang at the wark". Traditional, still heard in the North and Borders.
thrapple | Throat or windpipe: "grabbed him by the thrapple". Everyday among older speakers.
thraw | To twist; to be contrary. "Thrawn" (stubborn) is the more common form today.
thrawn | Stubborn, contrary. Very common for awkward people.
thrid | Third. Everyday among older speakers.
ticht | Tight; also stingy ("ticht wi his money"). Everyday.
tirrivee | A tantrum or fit of temper. Everyday and expressive.
tocher | Dowry. Old; you'll meet it in songs and stories.
tollie | A piece of poo (rude).
traivel | Travel; in older speech it simply means "to walk" – careful with that.
tuith | Tooth. Everyday among older speakers; "a sair tuith" = toothache.
twa | Two: "twa o them". Everyday everywhere.
twal | Twelve. Everyday among older speakers; Glasgow says "twelve".
um | Him, in fast unstressed speech ("gie it tae um"). Don't write it when "him" is stressed.
unco | Strange; very ("unco guid" = very good, or self-righteous).
unner | Under: "unner the bed". Everyday everywhere.
veesit | Visit: "a wee veesit". Everyday among older speakers.
wa | Wall: "ower the wa". Everyday; "waw" in Glasgow.
wabbit | Exhausted, done in. Very common and friendly; stronger than "tired".
wad | Would: "wad ye like tea?". Everyday everywhere; "widnae" = wouldn't.
wae | Woe, sorrow; "wae's me" = woe is me. Don't confuse with Glasgow "wae" (with).
waesucks | Alas. Old; mostly in poems.
waff | To wave or flap: "waff yer hanky". Traditional.
wale | To choose; also the pick of something. Traditional.
ware | Spring (season), or seaweed. Mostly in the North and Islands.
wast | West: "the wast end". Everyday among older speakers.
watter | Water. Everyday; in Glasgow said WA'-ur with a glottal stop. "Doon the watter" = a day trip down the Clyde.
wean | A child – the Glasgow word. Elsewhere "bairn".
wee | Small, little. Also used for a little amount: "a wee while".
weel | Well: "weel done", "Ah'm weel". Everyday everywhere.
weel-faured | Good-looking, well-favoured. Traditional; nice in writing.
weird | Fate: "dree yer weird" = put up with your lot. Not "strange".
wha | Who: "wha's like us?". Everyday in the East and North; Glasgow says "who".
whan | When. Everyday in the East and North; Glasgow says "when".
whaur | Where: "whaur are ye?". Everyday everywhere.
whiles | Sometimes: "whiles ah wunner". Everyday; don't confuse with English "whilst".
wi | With: "come wi me". Glasgow speech often says "wae" or "wi"; both fine. Don't confuse "wae" (with) with "wae" (woe).
wife | Any woman, not just a married one: "an auld wife".
worry | In Scots it can mean to choke: "he worried on a bane". Not anxiety in this sense.
wrang | Wrong: "that's wrang". Everyday everywhere.
wullcat | Wildcat. Everyday in the country.
wyte | Blame: "it's no ma wyte", "dinnae wyte me". Everyday in the North and East.
yaird | Garden or yard. Everyday; "back yaird".
yammer | To whine or moan on. Everyday.
ye | You (one person or more). Glasgow uses "yous/yiz/yeez" for more than one; "ye" is the general Scots form.
yeld | Barren, not giving milk (of animals). Farming word.
yer | Your. Everyday everywhere: "yer maw".
yersel | Yourself: "look efter yersel". Everyday everywhere.
yestreen | Last night. Traditional.
yett | Gate. Traditional; still in place names.
yill | Ale. Traditional; you'll meet it in songs.
yird | Earth, soil; to bury. Traditional.
yirdit | Buried. Traditional.
yowe | Ewe. Everyday in farming areas.
Yule | Christmas. Traditional; still used in the North and in songs.
thir | "These" – things near the speaker. Pairs with "thae" (those, further away). Glasgow often uses "these" or "they" instead; "thir" is more traditional and East/North.
thae | "Those" – things further away. Pairs with "thir" (these). Glasgow commonly says "they" (they shoes) or "thon"; "thae" is general Scots.
thon | "That/those over there" – everyday Glasgow and West. "Thon wan" = that one. Interchangeable with "yon", which sounds more traditional or rural.
yon | "That/those over there", for something at a distance. Traditional and rural; in Glasgow "thon" is more common.
yonner | "Over there", "yonder". Everyday in speech: "it's ower yonner". Use as an adverb, not before a noun (use "yon/thon" for that).
shite | Rude but very common in Glasgow and all over Scotland: rubbish ("that's shite"), nonsense ("talkin shite") or poo. Rhymes with "kite". Fine among pals; keep it out of polite company. "In the shite" = in big trouble.
